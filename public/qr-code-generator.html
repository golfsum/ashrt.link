Static and dynamic codes, and why it matters

A static QR code has your destination baked into the pattern. Once it is printed, that URL is the code, permanently. If the page moves, the campaign ends, or somebody mistypes the link before it goes to print, the only fix is a reprint.

A dynamic code encodes a short link instead, and the short link points at your destination. The pattern never changes; where it goes is a setting. Every code made here is dynamic, on the free plan, because a code that cannot be corrected is a liability on anything that costs money to print.

Scans are counted separately from clicks

Codes generated here carry a marker, so a scan is recorded as a scan rather than blending into the clicks from the same link shared in an email. You get both numbers, and the marker is never passed on to your destination.

Designing one that still scans

Most "designed" QR codes fail for the same three reasons, and they are all avoidable:

  • The quiet zone was removed. The empty margin around the pattern is part of the code. Four modules is the specification's number, and cropping to the edge is the most common reason a code stops scanning.
  • The corner squares were restyled. A scanner finds those three squares first, by their 1:1:3:1:1 ratio. Rounding their corners is fine. Changing their proportions is not.
  • Contrast was lost. A light pattern on a dark background, or two colours of similar brightness, reads badly under shop lighting. Dark pattern, light background, high contrast.

A logo in the middle is fine if the code is generated at the highest error-correction level, which can lose about 30% of the pattern and still read. Our paid plans set that automatically when you add a logo, along with colours, dot and corner styles, a frame and a caption. Every combination is verified by rendering it and decoding it again, not by looking at it.

Print sizes that work

The usual rule is that a code should be about a tenth of the distance it will be scanned from. Roughly: 2cm across for something held in the hand, 5cm for a table tent or a menu, 10cm for a poster read from a metre away, and considerably larger for a window or a banner.
